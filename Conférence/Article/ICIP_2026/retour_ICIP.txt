----------Reviewer #2----------
Author Feedback
This paper focuses on a specialized task: picking a single, most representative "tag-image" from a cinematic shot. Rather than relying on simple frame selection, the authors use an object detector to create class-occurrence histograms and then apply a triple-weighting system: spatial, temporal, and sharpness to find the frame that best matches the shot's overall semantic content.
Strengths:
1/ Expert-Collaborative Framework: By working with film scholars to define "tag-images" as distinct from standard key-frames, the authors show a deep understanding of the specific requirements for cinematic analysis.
2/ Narrative-Aware Temporal Weighting: The "Double Gaussian" function is a clever, mathematically sound way to prioritize frames near a shot's middle or end where narrative climaxes typically occur.
3/ High-Level Human Validation: Utilizing 16 film analysis experts to rank the extracted images gives the results a level of qualitative authority that is often missing in purely automated studies.
4/ Rigorous Methodological Testing: The ablation study effectively proves that each component - spatial, temporal, and sharpness is necessary to achieve the reported performance gains.

Areas for Improvement
1/ Outdated Technical Backbone: Using Faster R-CNN (a 2017 architecture) as the core detector is a significant drawback for a 2026 conference. Without a comparison against modern, transformer-based or real-time detectors like YOLOv10, the technical approach feels quite dated.
2/ Insufficient Validation Scale: While the expert feedback is valuable, performing that study on only 10 film shots is a very limited sample size. It’s hard to claim these rules are robust when they've only been tested on such a small fraction of cinematic styles.
3/ Incremental Innovation: The core methodology builds very directly on the work of Chen et al.. Moving to histogram-based statistics is a logical step, but it feels like a relatively minor refinement rather than a major shift in the field.
4/ Narrative Generalization: The temporal weighting assumes a standard narrative arc for every shot. The paper would be much stronger if it addressed how this weighting might fail in non-traditional cinema, such as experimental films or documentaries that don't follow a climax-based structure.
Notation Consistency: There are minor errors in the manuscript

While the interdisciplinary focus and expert involvement are great, the paper is technically too thin for a high-level vision conference. The combination of an outdated detector and a tiny 10-shot validation set means the work doesn't yet provide the rigorous proof needed for a definite accept. To be successful, the authors need to modernize their backbone and test on a much wider variety of film shots.



----------Reviewer #3----------
Author Feedback
The manuscript presents a histogram-based method for extracting a representative tag-image from individual film shots. The proposed approach models the semantic content of frames using object detection outputs and selects the frame whose histogram is closest to the average histogram of the shot. The method is further enhanced with spatial, temporal, and sharpness weightings inspired by cinematographic principles.
The paper addresses an interesting problem in multimedia analysis related to video summarization and film indexing. The methodology is clearly described and the evaluation includes both quantitative metrics and expert-based qualitative assessment. Overall, the work represents a useful contribution; however, several aspects could be improved to strengthen the clarity and positioning of the study.

Strengths
- The paper addresses a relevant task in multimedia analysis: automatic extraction of representative frames from film shots.
- The histogram-based representation of object detection outputs provides an interpretable semantic modeling of frame content.
- The spatial, temporal, and sharpness weightings are well motivated by cinematic principles.
- The evaluation includes both objective metrics and expert-based assessment, which is appropriate given the subjective nature of the task.

Paper Weaknesses
• Positioning of the contribution.
The proposed approach combines histogram statistics with spatial, temporal, and sharpness weightings. While effective, the manuscript could clarify more explicitly how this strategy differs conceptually from existing key-frame extraction methods based on object detection or feature aggregation.

• Dataset scale.
The evaluation is conducted on relatively small datasets (105 annotated shots and a second dataset of 10 shots evaluated by experts). While the expert evaluation is valuable, a brief discussion on how the method may generalize to larger or more diverse video collections would strengthen the work.

• Evaluation metrics.
The evaluation mainly relies on the Bhattacharyya distance between histograms. While appropriate for histogram comparison, discussing alternative similarity measures could further support the robustness of the evaluation.

• Parameter discussion.
The parameters used for spatial and temporal weighting are reported but not extensively discussed. A brief justification or discussion of their selection would improve methodological transparency.



----------Reviewer #4----------
Author Feedback
The proposed method is histogram-based over detected classes for semantic consistency to video tag-image/key-image summarization (the representative image of the sequence). The authors propose a centered spatial weighting and temporal weights to capture climaxes and then attempt to align computational extraction with expert based film analysis. This is interesting for sure, since it uses an evaluation involving 16 experts - something refreshing than purely data-driven methods that ignore artistry in cinema and movie-making.
However, there are some statistical weaknesses to the approach. It seems that the approach is an incremental extension to Chen et al., relying to standard heuristics like Laplacian variance. Also the central assumptions seem a little oversimplified (e.g. central objects are more relevant). Also, there is no discussion on how some of these technique would fail – maybe for art films or avant-garde films that sometimes break convention with long takes or uncommon action sequences.
The experimental validation also seems a little weak. The main quantitative evaluation uses 105 shots with ground truth from a single expert; the authors do run a second study with 16 experts, but on only 10 shots - too few to substantiate claims about robustness across diverse shot types; a strict ranking or inter-rater agreement on a unique best frame would provide stronger evidence. The paper also does not report reliability or disagreement among experts, so the multi-expert study complements the first experiment but is not a substitute for dense, per-shot multi-annotator ground truth. Overall, the evidence supports relative comparisons under these narrow conditions but does not yet establish a robust or broad application beyond the tested settings.



----------Reviewer #5----------
Author Feedback
This paper tackles the problem of selecting a representative frame (referred to as a tag-image) for a given film shot, which is an interesting and relatively underexplored topic in the context of film analysis. The motivation is well presented, especially considering the diversity and subjectivity inherent to cinematic content.
The proposed method is simple, intuitive, and easy to follow. It relies on representing each frame using object-based histograms and selecting the frame that best matches the average representation of the shot. The inclusion of spatial, temporal, and sharpness weightings is well justified and aligns with common cinematic principles, such as central composition, narrative progression, and visual quality. While each component is standard, their combination is meaningful and adapted to the task. The ablation study also provides useful insights into the contribution of each component.
The experimental results indicate that the proposed method performs better than the compared baselines, including in an evaluation involving human experts. This is a valuable aspect, as the notion of representativeness is inherently subjective and benefits from human assessment.
That said, the overall level of technical novelty is relatively limited. The approach mainly combines existing techniques (object detection, histogram-based representations, and heuristic weightings) rather than introducing fundamentally new ideas. In addition, the experimental validation could be strengthened. The multi-expert evaluation is conducted on only 10 shots, which may not be sufficient to fully capture the variability of cinematic content. Moreover, experts are allowed to select multiple images per shot, which may inflate selection rates and reduce the discriminative power of the evaluation. The first dataset relies on a single expert annotation, which may introduce bias. Furthermore, more details regarding the evaluation protocol and expert agreement would improve the reliability of the conclusions.

In summary, the paper presents a clear and practically relevant approach, but the contribution remains incremental and would benefit from a stronger and more comprehensive evaluation.
